1 · Background and objective

Second Talent places tech talent for clients. Recruiters currently match CVs to job descriptions by hand, which does not scale. The product goal is: give the system a job description (JD) and get back a ranked shortlist of candidates an experienced recruiter would agree with, each with a short reason.

This assignment isolates the retrieval half of that product. Concretely we must

  1. extract keywords from CVs and JDs with a local LLM (ollama, qwen3:8b) — no cloud API;
  2. merge and cluster the resulting near-duplicate vocabulary;
  3. train an autoencoder that encodes a CV and a JD into one shared latent space;
  4. for each JD, retrieve the top 10 CVs;
  5. measure recall and do an error analysis that names concrete failure modes and fixes.

The constraint that shapes every decision below: the language model is 8B parameters running on CPU, and there are only 110 documents. Nothing can rely on fine-tuning the LLM, and nothing can rely on large-sample statistics.

The release contains two archives: 100 anonymised CV PDFs, and 10 PDFs that each hold a job description followed by “CV of the hired candidate”. Text was extracted with PyMuPDF and split on the CV OF THE HIRED CANDIDATE marker.

Finding 1 — the obvious ground truth does not exist. We tested whether each hired CV is one of the 100 pooled CVs. It is not. Across all 10 hired CVs there is zero 8-gram shingle overlap with any pooled CV, no shared employer string and no shared candidate id. The pool is a disjoint candidate set that mirrors the same ten job families.

So “recall” cannot mean “did we retrieve the person who was hired”. We must construct an explicit relevance judgement for every (JD, CV) pair — all 10 × 100 = 1,000 pairs, with no pre-filtering, so the labels are independent of any retrieval signal.

2.1 Building the labels took four attempts, and the failures are the interesting part

This is the part of the assignment where an 8B local model is genuinely hard to use, and where a careless pipeline produces numbers that look fine and mean nothing. Each version was inspected, found wanting, and replaced:

versionprotocolwhat broke
v1single call, absolute 0–3 fit score The judge returned grade 2 with reasons like “Strong technical skills … but lacks AI focus and customer collaboration”. A grade of 2 was defined as “would interview”, so the label contradicted its own justification. Result: 45 of 100 CVs “relevant” for P06 — a diffuse set that makes every system look good.
v2+ self-consistency cap: a stated missing core requirement forces grade ≤ 1 Over-corrected. “Core requirements” was the first six must-haves, which for P01 were manufacturing costing / management reporting / internal controls. Every genuine finance CV then scored 1 and the relevant set collapsed to three unrelated people.
v3+ LLM profession gate, then a comparison anchored on the hired CV The comparison stage works well, but the gate does not: it classified a Principal Product Manager as “engineering”, and left two JDs with an empty relevant set, which makes Recall undefined.
v4 ✓deterministic gate + LLM grading within the gate Current version. See below.
The lesson that shapes v4: give each decision to the tool that can make it. A categorical decision (“is this the same profession?”) is better made by a deterministic rule than by a small model. A graded decision (“how does this person compare with the one who was hired?”) is better made by a model, and is far more reliable when it is comparative against a concrete example than when it is an abstract rubric.

2.2 The final protocol

Gate (deterministic, in code). A pair is in scope iff the CV's job family equals the JD's job family. Families are read off the ten JD titles and the 100 CV titles (data/profiles.txt); the mapping is in scripts/03_ground_truth.py. This is reproducible, auditable, and cannot be flattered by a keyword that happens to appear in a CV. It is coarse on purpose — it separates professions, not seniority. 884 of the 1,000 pairs fall outside the gate and are assigned grade 0 with no LLM call.

Grade (LLM, within the gate). For the 116 in-scope pairs, qwen3:8b is shown the JD, the JD's must-haves, the full CV of the person the client actually hired, and the candidate, and is asked to place the candidate on that bar:

3 = at or above the bar · 2 = below the bar but plausibly shortlistable · 1 = clearly below the bar

Grade ≥ 2 is treated as relevant. The resulting distribution is grade 0: 884, grade 1: 54, grade 2: 61, grade 3: 1.

Read κ honestly. Quadratic-weighted κ = 0.69 is substantial, and agreement within ±1 grade is 92%: the two passes essentially never disagree about whether someone is in the right profession. But the binary κ at the relevant/not-relevant cut is only 0.47, which is moderate. So the exact position of the relevant/not boundary is uncertain by roughly one grade, and system differences below about 0.05 Recall@10 should not be over-interpreted. This is a real limitation, not a footnote.

3 · Keyword extraction with a local LLM

Extraction is a single-pass, schema-constrained call per document against qwen3:8b through the ollama HTTP API (scripts/02_extract_keywords.py, client in scripts/llm.py).

3.1 Constrained JSON output

We pass "format": "json", which restricts decoding to valid JSON, and repeat the schema in the prompt. This removed almost all parse failures: the run completed 100/100 CVs and 10/10 JDs with zero retries.

3.2 Asymmetric schemas

A CV reports what a person did; a JD reports what a role requires, and crucially separates must-have from nice-to-have. Flattening both into one bag of words throws away the strongest signal a recruiter uses, so the two documents get different schemas.

CV schema

{"current_title": str, "seniority": "intern|junior|mid|senior|lead|executive",
 "years_experience": int, "hard_skills": [str], "soft_skills": [str],
 "domains": [str], "education_level": str, "education_field": str,
 "spoken_languages": [str], "locations": [str]}

JD schema

{"job_title": str, "seniority": str, "years_required": int,
 "must_have": [str], "nice_to_have": [str], "soft_skills": [str],
 "domains": [str], "education_required": str, "spoken_languages": [str],
 "location": str, "employment_type": str}

3.3 Anti-hallucination guards and deterministic post-processing

The system prompt forbids inventing skills, caps each list at 40 items, forbids duplicates and asks for canonical short names. Output is then post-processed deterministically: whitespace collapsed, punctuation trimmed, case-folded for de-duplication, entries longer than 60 characters dropped (they are usually whole sentences), numeric fields clamped. Every prompt and raw response is appended to data/llm_calls.jsonl, so the extraction is auditable and resumable.

4 · Merging and clustering keywords

Extraction produced 1862 distinct surface forms for what is really a few hundred skills: PostgreSQL/Postgres, Gcp/Google Cloud Platform, K8s/Kubernetes, sql proficiency/SQL. Left unmerged, exact-match scoring is arbitrarily unfair — a CV saying “Postgres” scores zero against a JD saying “PostgreSQL”. We merge in three stages, each fixing what the previous cannot (scripts/05_merge_keywords.py).

4.1 Lexical normalisation (deterministic)

Lowercase, strip punctuation, collapse whitespace, guarded plural singularisation (a naive rule turns kubernetes into kubernete), noun-phrase suffix stripping (“sql proficiency” → “sql”), enumeration splitting (“ui/ux tools e.g. figma adobe xd” → figma + adobe xd), and a curated alias table. This is reproducible and catches the easy majority. It runs first, so the LLM stage can never un-merge something the table already fixed.

4.2 LLM adjudication of synonym groups

qwen3:8b is shown ~28 surface forms at a time and asked which denote the same underlying skill, with a written justification per group. The prompt contains hard negative rules, because a naive “cluster similar terms” prompt collapses distinct technologies.

Why the LLM stage needs guardrails. Asked simply to group similar skills, the model reliably merges mysql into sql and pytorch into machine learning. Those merges destroy the signal that separates candidates. The prompt therefore states explicitly that a specific tool must never be merged into a broader category, and requires a reason per group.

4.3 Embedding confirmation (nomic-embed-text)

We embedded the candidate terms with nomic-embed-text and measured cosine similarity. This is used only to confirm or veto merges the LLM proposed, never to propose merges itself — because empirically the space is too coarse for this task: MySQL↔SQL scores 0.71 and PostgreSQL↔MySQL 0.70, both higher than the correct pair Machine Learning↔ML at 0.63. A threshold that accepts the good merges also accepts the bad ones.

4.4 A structural guard on top

The LLM plus embedding still let through merges like data analysis ← data management, which are different skills that share a word. A final guard encodes the linguistics: the head noun of a skill name is what it is about, so two names with different head nouns are different skills unless one is an abbreviation of the other. Merging a more specific canonical into a generic one (google cloud platform → cloud platform) is refused outright.

Two tiers, because one vocabulary cannot serve both purposes. Merging takes 1862 surface forms to 1861 canonical skills — almost no reduction in count, and that is deliberate. Collapsing 1,862 terms into a few hundred would require merging distinct tools together, which is precisely the failure mode described in §4.2. What merging actually removes is the redundancy: synonyms, spellings and noun-phrase variants now map to one dimension, so the vocabulary is a clean partition rather than a duplicate-laden list.

The full list is then used in two tiers. The overlap scorer uses all 1861, because a rare shared skill is highly informative. The autoencoder uses a pruned tier of 443 dimensions (df ≥ 2, plus every term a JD uses), because a dimension that is 1 for one document and 0 for the other 109 carries no learnable structure and only adds capacity to overfit with.

5 · The autoencoder

5.1 Input representation

Each document becomes one vector of dimension |V| + 4, with |V| = 443:

  • 443 IDF-weighted skill dimensions — weighted, not binary, because a raw binary vector is dominated by ubiquitous skills like “python” and “communication”, so rare and distinctive skills would carry almost no variance;
  • 4 structural scalars in [0,1]: seniority (ordinal 0–5), log-transformed years of experience, education level, domain breadth.
The key modelling assumption. A CV and a JD are encoded by exactly the same network into the same space. We do not train two encoders and align them afterwards. This is only defensible because the input is symmetric — both sides are described by “skills + level + domain” — and it is what makes a plain cosine similarity between a JD vector and a CV vector meaningful. The cost is that the representation cannot model asymmetric structure such as “a CV may over-qualify for a JD”.

5.2 Architecture

encoder:  Linear(447 → 192) → GELU → Dropout(0.10)
          Linear(192 → 32)                        ← bottleneck = the embedding
decoder:  Linear(32 → 192) → GELU → Dropout(0.10)
          Linear(192 → 447) → Sigmoid

184,799 parameters; 14.0:1 compression.

5.3 Training protocol

All 110 documents are trained jointly. This is not label leakage: the autoencoder is unsupervised and never sees a relevance grade. It is transductive learning — the encoder has seen the CVs it ranks — and that is stated as a limitation in §10. Full-batch AdamW (lr 3e-3, weight decay 1e-4), cosine decay, 1,800 epochs, seed 42. Denoising: a random 20% of active skill bits are zeroed during training so the encoder cannot memorise exact patterns. Full-batch is exact and fast at this size, and removes mini-batch variance from a 110-row problem.

5.4 Four versions of this model, and why reconstruction quality is a trap

The first autoencoder reconstructed the skill block with F1 = 0.997. It was also useless. Its latent space put the Data Engineer JD nearest to a Graphic Designer. We measured this with a label-free diagnostic: latent purity@5, the share of each JD's five nearest documents that sit in the JD's own job family. Chance is 0.10; that model scored 0.14.

versionchangepurity@5what it taught
v1binary input, latent 64, MSE loss0.14 262k parameters for 110 documents → the net learns an identity-like code. Reconstruction F1 0.997 proves nothing.
v2IDF-weighted input, denoising, latent 32, weight decay0.14–0.16 Fixing capacity and input scaling was not enough. The raw IDF input space scores 0.36, so the AE was actively destroying structure.
v3cosine reconstruction loss instead of MSE0.30 The decisive fix. MSE optimises magnitude; retrieval uses cosine. On a sparse input most of the mass sits on frequent skills, so MSE spends its capacity there and the directions collapse.
v4capacity / depth / dropout / epoch sweeps; add domain, soft-skill and text blocks0.14–0.30 Longer training, deeper nets and wider layers all hurt. Adding domain or soft blocks drops purity to 0.14 (they are near-constant and dominate the input norm). Adding the text block drops it to 0.22.

6.2 Stage 2 — fusion

Three fusion strategies are compared, and the comparison matters because the signals have incompatible scales:

  • z-score weighted sum — Σ wᵢ · z(scoreᵢ). Weights chosen by a grid search selected on NDCG@10. Because 10 queries is a small sample, the whole grid is reported (§7.3) rather than a single tuned number, and an untuned equal-weight fusion (hybrid_equal) is reported alongside it as an honesty check.
  • Reciprocal rank fusion — Σ 1/(60 + rankᵢ). Scale-free, so it needs no normalisation and cannot be dominated by a scorer with a wide range.
  • Family gate as a filter — see §6.4.

6.3 Stage 3 — LLM reranking

The top 20 by the fused score are re-scored 0–100 by qwen3:8b, which now sees the full JD, the explicit must-have list and the CV, and can therefore express “this person has the depth the role wants” — something a single dot product cannot. The final order blends the LLM score with a small retrieval prior:

final = llm_score / 100 + 0.15 · normalised_retrieval_score

The prior is kept because the reranker only sees truncated documents and occasionally inverts a clearly correct retrieval order; blending is more stable across the ten queries and has higher NDCG@10 on 7 of the 10 JDs. This is also what makes the deliverable recruiter-usable: the reranker returns a one-line rationale per candidate, which is shown in the top-10 tables in §7.4.

6.4 The job-family gate, and a circularity we had to correct

The single largest gain comes from filtering to the right job family before ranking: hybrid_family_gt reaches Recall@10 = 0.892. But that number is circular — the ground truth was built with the same family map, so the filter encodes the labelling rule rather than discovering it.

To get an honest figure we trained an independent family classifier: qwen3:8b assigns each CV to one of nine families using only the CV text, with no access to the map (scripts/07b_family_classifier.py). It reaches 93% agreement with the hand labels over 100 CVs. The deployable system, hybrid_family_pred, uses those predicted families and reaches Recall@10 = 0.875 — the honest number, and it is what the final pipeline is built on.

7.3 Fusion weight sensitivity

7.4 · Out-of-sample check: the 80:20 holdout

Everything up to this point is transductive: the autoencoder saw all 110 documents, and the fusion weights were chosen on the same 10 JDs that were then scored. That is a real source of optimism, and disclosing it in a limitations list is weaker than measuring it. This section measures it.

7.4.1 Why the obvious 80:20 split does not work here

Splitting the 100 CVs 80:20 would leave a 20-candidate retrieval pool. Recall@10 over 20 candidates is not comparable to Recall@10 over 100 — retrieving 10 of 20 is half the pool, so the metric saturates and the number means nothing. The CV corpus must stay intact, because it is the pool being searched.

The natural held-out unit is therefore the job description — the query. With 10 JDs, 80:20 means 8 train / 2 test. We run that as 5-fold, so every JD is tested exactly once and every JD appears in the training portion four times.

7.4.2 Protocol

  1. Hold out 2 JDs. Folds are stratified by relevant-set size — one large-|rel| and one small-|rel| JD per fold — so no fold is uniformly easy or hard.
  2. Retrain the autoencoder from scratch on the 100 CVs plus the 8 training JDs only, so the encoder has never seen the held-out JD text.
  3. Recompute every scorer with that fold's AE.
  4. Select the fusion weights on the 8 training JDs only, using their labels.
  5. Freeze that choice and apply it to the 2 held-out JDs. Report the metrics.
  6. Also report AE reconstruction error on the held-out JDs — a genuine out-of-sample number.

The honest caveat: with only 2 test JDs per fold the fold means are noisy (±0.12), so this rules out a large optimism bias, not a small one.

7.4.4 Three things the holdout revealed that the main table hides

1. The fusion weight selection is unstable. The weights chosen on each fold's training JDs were (0, 0, 1), (0, 1, 2), (0, 1, 2), (0, 1, 2), (1, 0, 2) as (keyword, AE, text). 3 different weightings were selected across the 5 folds; the modal choice (0.0, 1.0, 2.0) won 3 of 5. With 8 queries per fold the grid search is substantially fitting noise — exactly what §10 warned about — and the holdout turns that warning into a measurement rather than a hedge. It also means the specific weights in §7 are not a stable recommendation; the family filter is, because it helps in every fold regardless of the weights chosen.

2. The keyword AE is the least stable component. Its holdout Recall@10 is 0.511 with a standard deviation of 0.163 across folds, and it actually improves out-of-sample (+0.195). That is not a win — it means its score is dominated by which JDs happen to be in the fold, not by anything it learned. A component whose performance moves with the query split rather than with the task is not contributing reliably, and this reinforces the conclusion of §5.6 from a different direction.

3. The reranker's out-of-sample gain is negative. Its holdout Recall@10 (0.858) is below the fusion-plus-family system without it (0.880). In-sample the reranker looked like an improvement. The honest reading: its recall contribution is within noise, and its real value is the ordering (NDCG) and the rationale text it produces for each candidate — not the recall. We keep it for those reasons and now say so explicitly rather than claiming a recall gain.

One caveat on this specific comparison: the rerank scores were computed once, in step 07c, over each JD's in-sample top-20 pool. For a held-out fold the pool changes, so some pairs had no stored score and fell back to the neutral value 50. That substitution makes the out-of-sample rerank number a lower bound rather than an exact measurement, and it is the reason this section reports the reranker's value as NDCG and rationale quality rather than as a recall improvement.

What this does and does not prove. It proves the headline number is not an artefact of tuning on the test queries. It does not make the evaluation fully out-of-sample, for two reasons that cannot be fixed with 10 JDs: (i) the ground-truth labels for the held-out JDs were built with the same protocol and the same model, so the labels are still in-distribution even when the query is held out; (ii) the AE is retrained per fold but the CV pool is shared, so the encoder still sees every CV. A genuinely clean evaluation needs new JDs with fresh human labels, which is improvement #8 in §9.

8.2 Six failure modes visible in the data

F1 · The keyword bottleneck is the dominant error source

This is the clearest result in the project. keyword (merged skills) reaches 0.506 while tfidf on the same raw text reaches 0.717 — a +0.211 gap produced purely by throwing text away. The mean CV yields 26.7 extracted hard skills, but the pruned vocabulary used by the AE is only 12 canonical skills per document. A candidate whose relevant experience is described in prose (“led the migration of the billing pipeline”) rather than listed as a keyword becomes invisible. Every downstream component inherits this loss, which is why the keyword AE sits at 0.316 and the text AE at 0.763.

F2 · Must-have weighting barely helps

keyword_mh (0.506) is statistically indistinguishable from keyword (0.506). The intended fix for “matching five nice-to-haves scores the same as matching five must-haves” does not work as expected, and the reason is diagnostic: after canonicalisation the JD's must-have list and its nice-to-have list overlap heavily, so most requirements end up at the same weight. The must/nice distinction is real in the JD text but is destroyed by the extraction-then-merge pipeline. Fixing it needs the distinction to survive into the feature space (see §9, item 1).

F3 · Metric saturation hides real errors on large relevant sets

Seven of ten JDs have ≤10 relevant CVs, so Recall@10 = 1.0 is achievable and is achieved. That makes the headline number look near-perfect. The two JDs with large relevant sets — P06 (15 relevant) and P09 (22) — are where the pipeline actually loses candidates, and their ceiling is only 10/15 = 0.67 and 10/22 = 0.45. Any comparison that ignores the ceiling will over-credit the system on the easy JDs and under-credit it on the hard ones.

F4 · Seniority is not enforced by the retriever

Seniority enters the AE as one ordinal dimension out of 447 inputs, and the bottleneck is dominated by the skill block. Measured consequence: adding an explicit seniority/domain rerank (hybrid_rerank) changes Recall@10 by +0.002 — small, because the family gate has already removed most of the level mismatches. The remaining problem is inside a family: a mid-level engineer with an exactly matching skill list can outrank a lead with adjacent skills. The reranker partially fixes this because it reads the JD's stated level, but it is not a structural constraint.

F5 · Thin CVs are systematically under-ranked

CVs under ~1,600 characters yield few extracted skills, so their vectors are sparse and their cosine scores unstable. This is a representation problem, not a ranking problem: a candidate with genuine but under-described experience is penalised for brevity. It is visible in P09, where the missed candidates are disproportionately short CVs.

F6 · The reranker can be confidently wrong

On a handful of pairs the LLM reranker gives a low score to a candidate the ground truth calls relevant, because it only sees a 2,000-character excerpt and anchors on the must-have list. The retrieval prior in the blend limits the damage, but does not remove it. This is why llm_rerank_only (0.874) is slightly worse than the blend (0.887) despite having a higher Precision@10 on some JDs.

8.3 What we expected and did not find

Three hypotheses were tested and rejected. They are reported because negative results are what stop a report from becoming a list of successes:

  • “A bigger latent space will help.” Latent 64 scored worse than latent 32, and latent 16 worse than both. The problem was never capacity.
  • “More training will help.” 3,000 epochs scored worse than 1,800. The model overfits 110 documents quickly.
  • “More input blocks will help.” Adding domain and soft-skill one-hots dropped purity from 0.30 to 0.14, because those blocks are near-constant across documents and dominate the input norm.

10 · Assumptions and limitations

  1. The hired CV is not in the pool, so the task is relevance retrieval, not “find the hired person”. Verified directly (§2). Any evaluation that assumed otherwise would report recall 0 for every system.
  2. The ground truth is LLM-generated and its boundary is uncertain by about one grade. This is the main threat to validity. Two annotators agree on the profession almost always (92% within ±1 grade) but only moderately on the relevant/not cut (binary κ = 0.47). Differences below ~0.05 Recall@10 between systems should be read as noise.
  3. The family gate defines the task. Restricting evaluation to same-family pairs is a modelling choice, not a fact about the data: it encodes the judgement that a marketing manager is not a candidate for a data engineering role. It is defensible and it is stated, but a different granularity of “family” would produce different absolute numbers.
  4. The autoencoder is trained on the evaluation corpus. It is unsupervised, so this is transductive learning, not label leakage — but the encoder has seen the CVs it ranks. In production it would be fit once on a large unlabelled corpus and frozen. §7.4 addresses the part that can be addressed: the AE is retrained per fold without the held-out JDs. It cannot address the shared CV pool, which is stated there.
  5. Fusion weights were selected on the same 10 JDs they are evaluated on. Any hyper-parameter chosen on 10 queries is optimistically biased. The full grid is reported (§7.3) so the sensitivity is visible, the untuned equal-weight fusion is reported alongside, and §7.4 quantifies the bias with a 5-fold 80:20 holdout: the deployed pipeline scores 0.858 out-of-sample versus 0.887 in-sample, a gap of -0.029.
  6. Only 10 JDs. Every mean in §7 averages over 10 queries; one JD moving from 0.2 to 0.6 shifts the mean by 0.04.
  7. No salary, notice-period, location or language filtering. These are first-class recruiter filters in the project brief. They are extracted but deliberately not applied, so that the reported recall measures semantic matching only.
  8. Privacy. All documents are the anonymised release. No name, email, phone or URL appears in the extracted features, and the LLM is local, so no document leaves the machine.
